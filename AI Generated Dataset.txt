There are two main categories for sentiment classification techniques, which are machine learning-based approaches and lexicon-based approaches.
Machine learning methods are commonly used in sentiment analysis.
The Machine-learning approach can be classified into three methods: supervised, unsupervised, and semi-supervised.
In supervised sentiment analysis, a classifier is trained using a large dataset that has already been labeled with sentiments. The labeled data is used to teach the classifier how to recognize the sentiment of new, unlabeled data.
The key feature of labeled training documents in supervised sentiment analysis is that they contain topic-related words and express either a positive or negative opinion.
The following section briefly explains the sub-classifications of the Supervised Learning method: Probabilistic Classifier, Decision Tree, Linear classifier, and Rule-Based.
Decision trees are structured models used for classification or regression analysis, presented in a tree-like structure. They are often used for prediction purposes, and are particularly effective for classification tasks.
In the decision tree classification method, an unknown record with an unknown class label is given and the decision tree is tested on this record. The decision tree then follows the path from the root node to the node that determines the prediction of the class for the record.
These methods are popular because building a decision tree does not require any specific domain expertise or complex settings.
The ID3 algorithm is the primary method for constructing decision trees, which uses Information Gain (IG) and Entropy. ID3 and C5 algorithms are commonly used for implementing decision trees in text classification problems (Vryniotis et al., 2013).
Linear classifiers are known for their simplicity and ease of use.
Linear classifiers are commonly used to determine the polarity of a sentence by calculating the frequency of positive and negative opinion words in the comments and matching them with the sentence.
In linear classifiers, the sentiment of a sentence is determined by assigning weights to all the words present in the sentence, with the highest weight given to the most positive words and the lowest weight given to the most negative words. The total weight of the words present in the sentence is then used to determine the sentence's polarity.
The most commonly used linear-classifier in sentiment analysis is the Support Vector Machines (SVM) classifier (Medhat et al., 2014).
The Rule-Based Classifier and Decision Tree Classifier are similar in that they encode rules on the feature space.
While both the rule-based classifier and the decision tree classifier encode rules on the feature space, the main difference between them lies in their approach. The decision tree classifier uses a hierarchical approach, whereas the rule-based classifier allows for overlap in the decision space, as stated by Liu et al. in 2012.
Decision tree classifiers can be converted to rule-based classifiers in several ways. One common approach is to extract the decision rules from the decision tree by following each path from the root node to a leaf node. Another approach is to use decision tree induction algorithms that generate rules directly from the data, such as the RIPPER algorithm.
The rule-based classifier generates rules during the training phase, which are based on various conditions. The most commonly used methods for rule generation in this classifier are support and confidence, as stated by Pietquin et al. (2004).
Unsupervised machine-learning methods are often utilized in situations where acquiring labeled training documents is difficult or impossible.
The unsupervised learning method is primarily used when it is challenging to create class-labeled training documents.
Semi-supervised learning involves using a combination of labeled and unlabeled data for training, where a small set of labeled data is supplemented with a large amount of unlabeled data (Dorothy and Rajini, 2013).

The Lexicon-based method is another type of unsupervised approach, but it relies on utilizing a dictionary of opinionated words and phrases, along with their corresponding sentiment orientation, including synonyms and antonyms.
The lexicon-based method can be categorized into two sub-methods: dictionary-based and corpus-based. The dictionary-based method uses a pre-compiled dictionary of opinionated words and their corresponding sentiment orientation. The corpus-based method, on the other hand, employs either a semantic or statistical approach to determine the sentiment polarity of the input text.

Wireless Sensor Networks (WSNs) use radio waves to facilitate communication between nodes in a network, while Underwater Sensor Networks (UWSNs) rely on acoustic signals for communication between nodes.
Because radio signals experience rapid attenuation in underwater environments, they are not effective for communication. This is why acoustic signals are used in Underwater Sensor Networks (UWSNs) instead. Acoustic signals are preferred due to their reliability, ability to transmit or receive signals in all directions, and cost-effectiveness.

Underwater Sensor Networks (UWSNs) consist of two main components: sink nodes and underwater sensor nodes.
The underwater sensor nodes in UWSNs are designed to sense and collect data from the underwater environment. They use routing protocols to transmit the collected data to the sink node on the surface.
In UWSNs, there are two primary modes of transmission: direct transmission and multiple hop transmission.
When the path between an underwater sensor node and the sink node is straight, data can be directly transmitted without forwarding it to other neighbor nodes. However, when the path is not straight, such as in the case of obstacles or longer distances, hop-by-hop transmission is used. This involves forwarding the data from one node to another until it reaches the sink node on the surface.
There are two types of sinks used in UWSNs: static sinks and mobile sinks. Static sinks are typically installed on the water surface.
The main weakness of using a static sink in UWSNs is that the underwater sensor nodes located closer to the surface sink may die first due to an unbalanced energy load. This is because these nodes are responsible for forwarding more data packets than the nodes located at greater depths.
The unbalanced energy load caused by the closely deployed underwater sensor nodes to the surface sinks can create routing problems in UWSNs.
Using mobile sinks can be a better alternative to static sinks in UWSNs for obtaining information from underwater sensor nodes.

Image compression techniques reduce redundant data that is not perceptible to the human eye within a single frame.
This is accomplished through intraframe coding.
The video compression algorithm employs interframe coding to reduce redundant information across a sequence of frames.
In this technique, the current frame is compared to a reference frame, and the differences in the pixel values relative to the reference frame are encoded.
Therefore, only the reference frame and the difference values are transmitted, omitting the unchanged pixels. (Dennis et al. 2009)
Data can be further reduced through block-based motion compensation, which divides the current frame into macro-blocks and predicts the new frame by matching these blocks with the reference frame.
The position in the reference frame where a match is found is encoded by the encoder as a motion vector.
In inter-prediction mode, each frame can be classified as an I-frame, P-frame, or B-frame.
I-frame is an intraframe that does not require a reference frame for decoding. It is typically the first frame in a sequence and is essential for resynchronization in case any bits are corrupted during transmission.
P-frame is a predictive interframe that encodes frames by referencing the macroblocks from preceding I-frames or P-frames.
B-frame, known as a bi-predictive interframe, has increased latency and references both a previous reference frame and a future frame.
The encoded bit stream is decoded frame by frame to reconstruct the original video.

There are various video coding standards.
H.261 was the first video coding standard in the H.26x family, developed by the ITU-T.
Later, MPEG-1 was developed as an improved version of JPEG and H.261.
Modifications to MPEG-1 resulted in a newer version of video coding, MPEG-2, which is widely used as the format for digital television signals.

It was introduced by the ITU-T (International Telecommunication Union) in collaboration with MPEG.
It is one of the most recent and widely used video coding standards for the compression, recording, processing, and transmission of high-definition videos and is commonly used for Blu-ray discs.
H.264 offers 50% improved efficiency compared to previous formats and provides a network-friendly video representation. (Po-Chyi et al. 2017)
H.264/AVC is the advanced video coding standard developed by the ITU-T Video Coding Experts Group in collaboration with the ISO/IEC Moving Picture Experts Group.
H.264 is one of the latest standards that offers highly efficient video compression, a network-friendly video representation, and enhanced rate-distortion efficiency.
Advanced Video Coding (AVC) is based on a hybrid block-based motion compensation and transform-coding methodology, but it also includes several advanced features, such as tree-structured motion compensation with luminance (Y) block sizes of 16x16, 16x8, 8x16, and 8x8.
The 8x8 sub-macro block can be further partitioned into blocks of sizes 8x4, 4x8, and 4x4.
It supports motion-compensated prediction with multiple reference frames, achieving sub-pixel accuracy up to one-quarter of a pixel (1/4 pixel). Additionally, it features a deblocking filter that predicts pixel values from neighboring samples of already coded blocks.
It utilizes smaller block sizes (4x4) for integer transforms instead of the conventional 8x8 discrete cosine transform, which helps to minimize blocking artifacts in video frames.
An efficient Context Adaptive Variable Length Coding (CAVLC) mechanism is employed in the H.264 baseline profile. While these features of H.264 enhance the codec's efficiency in terms of quality, its computational complexity poses challenges for implementation. (Po-Chyi et al. 2017)

The Flower Pollination Algorithm (FPA) was developed by Xin-She Yang in 2012.
In nature, the primary purpose of plants is reproduction through fertilization.
Flower pollination involves the transfer of pollen, facilitated by pollinators such as birds, insects, bats, other animals, or the wind.
The primary goal of flower pollination is ultimately reproduction through the process of pollination.
Certain flower species have specific pollinators that are essential for successful pollination.
Four rules of pollination, inspired by flowering plants, have been established and form the foundational equations of the Flower Pollination Algorithm.
One type is cross-pollination, or biotic pollination, which occurs when pollen is transferred from the flower of one plant to the flower of a different plant.
In this type of pollination, pollen is transferred from one plant to another by pollinators such as birds, insects, and bats, accounting for about 90% of pollination occurrences.
This is also known as the global pollination method.
Self or abiotic pollination occurs when pollen comes from the same flower or from another flower on the same plant, accounting for about 10% of pollination events.
In this type of pollination, pollen is transferred from one plant to another via wind or water, and it is also referred to as local pollination.
The following steps are used in the Flower Pollination Algorithm:
Step 1: Global pollination, represented by biotic and cross-pollination methods, occurs as pollen-carrying pollinators move according to Lévy flight.
Step 2:Local pollination, represented by abiotic and self-pollination, is a process that does not require any pollinators.
Step 3:Flower reliability is determined by insects and is proportional to the reproduction potential, which correlates with the similarity between the two flowers involved.
Step 4:The interaction between local and global pollination is quantified by a switch probability \( p \in [0, 1] \), which is slightly biased toward local pollination.
